The data

There is no public sample-level turnaround dataset, so this one is simulated. I used real lab targets and real processes, and kept each sample's timestamps honest: every tube queues for the same people and machines as every other tube.

What is modelled

  • Five wards with their own order patterns, including the 06:00 morning bloods on the wards
  • Pneumatic tube from ED and ICU, porters elsewhere, one porter overnight
  • A reception rota with a handover gap, and urgent bags that are not always spotted
  • Two centrifuges that batch tubes, daily QC on the chemistry line, Wednesday maintenance
  • Haemolysed samples rejected and taken again, and results that need a scientist to release them
  • An unplanned pneumatic tube outage on Friday 16 October, 11:00 to 16:30

Targets

Urgent: result within 60 minutes of the order, the usual ED expectation for tests like troponin. Routine: within 4 hours.

How to read it

“What makes late samples late” compares the average minutes at each stage for late and on time samples. The stage with the biggest gap is where delay comes from, not just where time is spent.

1. Measure every stage

Order, collection, transport, booking in, centrifuge, analyser queue, assay and release. The slowest stage on average is the assay, but that's not where the late samples lose their time. Comparing late with on time shows the real bottleneck.

2. Questions to queries

A small parser turns a question like “why were urgent bloods from the wards late at night?” into filters and an intent: why, which ward, which test, what time. The answer shows exactly what it understood, so you can check it.

3. Test the fix first

A queueing simulation reruns the month with one change: an earlier reception shift, QC moved out of the morning peak, another porter or another centrifuge. Some fixes help a lot. Buying another centrifuge barely moves the number.
