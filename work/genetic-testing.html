01

The problem

A GP sees a child with heart defects and an eye problem. It might be genetic. But most GPs are not genetics specialists, and there are thousands of possible tests. Pick the wrong one and the family waits months for an answer that was never going to come.

There is a second problem. Genetic data is about the most sensitive data there is. A tool that touches it has to earn trust on privacy, not just on accuracy.

02

What I built

A GP enters the patient's symptoms. The tool ranks the most likely conditions, shows the genes behind them, and recommends one test: a single gene test, a targeted panel, or exome sequencing.

Around that sits everything a clinic would need: consent before anything runs, pseudonymised data, different views for a GP, a genetic counsellor, a lab and IT, a check before data goes overseas, and an audit trail chained with SHA-256 so any edited entry shows. You can switch each safeguard off and watch what breaks.

03

How it works

  1. Code the symptomsEach symptom is a Human Phenotype Ontology term, the standard vocabulary clinicians use.
  2. Weight rare symptoms moreA seizure appears in hundreds of conditions. A lens dislocation appears in a few. Rare symptoms narrow the search, so they count for more.
  3. Score every condition70% for how much of the patient it explains, 30% for how much of the condition's own profile the patient shows.
  4. Clean the data firstThe same condition was often listed twice, once from each database. Merging them stopped it splitting its own score.
  5. Recommend one testA clear leader with few genes gets a targeted test. An uncertain case gets a wider panel and a referral to a specialist.
04

What I found

I tested it on 500 practice patients per scenario, made from real disease profiles, where I already knew the right answer.

With 5 symptoms, the right condition came first 88% of the time, and the right gene was in the recommended test 99% of the time. Add 2 unrelated symptoms, the kind a busy GP might also note, and the top answer dropped to 41%. Messy input is the real risk.

The biggest find was the confidence label. It said High on 83% of cases but was right only 69% of the time. I rebuilt the rule from the test results. Now it says Clear lead on a third of cases and is right 88% of the time on cases it had never seen.

05

What I learned

The best fix came from a test, not a model.The overconfident label was invisible until I tested it like a clinician would.
Data cleaning beat the algorithm.Merging duplicate conditions changed results more than tuning the score.
What I'd do nextValidate on real clinical cases, use the full HPO vocabulary, and get regulatory review. It is a teaching prototype, not a medical device.
