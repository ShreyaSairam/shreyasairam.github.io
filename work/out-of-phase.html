01

The problem

Big batteries earn money by following the electricity price, which is set for a whole region. But the strain on the grid happens locally, at the substation on your street.

If the price peak and the local peak happen at different times, a battery chasing the price can make your street worse, not better.

02

What I built

A game and a lab built on real data. In the game, you run a real battery for one real day, one hour at a time: charge, hold or release, and try to earn money while keeping the street calm.

In the lab, you design your own plan by dragging when the battery charges and releases, compare Sydney with Delhi, and see the trade off between money and a calm street.

03

How it works

  1. Gather real dataSydney: AEMO market prices and 104 Ausgrid zone substations, every 15 minutes. Delhi: IEX exchange prices and Delhi SLDC city load.
  2. Draw the strain lineThe busiest 1% of each place's year marks a network at its hardest.
  3. Model the battery4 hours of storage, 90% round trip efficiency, starting and ending the day full.
  4. OptimiseFind the most money possible while keeping street strain under a chosen limit.
  5. Score the balanceCombine money kept and calm left on the street into one score out of 100.
04

What I found

Only 42% of the time did a Sydney substation's busiest hour land within an hour of the price peak. In summer, when it matters most, that fell to 24%. Delhi was 40%.

Chasing the price can leave the street worse off than having no battery at all. The cheapest time to charge is often midday, which is exactly when city centres are busiest.

05

What I learned

Turning research into a game works.People who would never read a report understood the problem in a minute by playing one day.
The optimiser shows a best case.It knows the whole day in advance. Real batteries have to forecast prices and load.
What would fix itPay batteries a small amount for releasing during local peaks, and give local network peaks a place in dispatch rules.
