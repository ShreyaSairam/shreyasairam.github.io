Stage 1 · Data

A million transactions become one row per customer per month

Raw bank data is a stream of transactions. Nobody can make a decision from that. The first job is to reshape it into a monthly view of every account: balance, money in, money out, where it went. Every bank's data team keeps a table like this.

Who does thisData engineering and the data warehouse team.Tools SQL, a warehouse (Snowflake, Teradata, BigQuery), scheduled jobs.Output a trusted, documented table other teams build on.

What can go wrong here
    
      
  • The public copy shifted every date by 20 years and divided amounts by 10. Left unfixed, every trend and amount would be wrong. The SQL restores both.
    • 
      
  • Months with no transactions disappear from a simple GROUP BY. A customer who goes quiet would vanish instead of showing up as a risk.
    • 
      
  • One account can have two holders. Joining on all of them double counts money, so the profile uses the owner only.

Stage 3 · Model

        
Predicting who will drift in the next three months

        
A gradient boosted tree model (LightGBM) looks at 23 signals per account and gives each one a probability of drifting. It is tested the way banks test: trained on the past, then scored on later months it has never seen.

      

      
Who does thisData science.Tools Python, scikit-learn, LightGBM, SHAP explanations.Output a model, its test results and an explanation for every score.

Stage 4 · Governance

        
No model reaches a customer until someone independent signs it off

        
Banks keep a separate team whose job is to challenge models: is it accurate, is it fair, is it legal, and will we notice when it stops working? This is the step most graduates have never heard of.

      

      
Who does thisModel risk management, the "second line".Tools validation tests, fairness checks, a model inventory.Output a model card and an approve, approve with conditions, or reject decision.

    

    

      

        
Model card

        

      

      

        
The three lines of defence

        

          
1st lineThe business that owns and uses the model. Here, retail banking.

          
2nd lineModel risk and compliance. Validate it independently and can say no.

          
3rd lineInternal audit. Checks that the first two did their jobs.

        

        

        

        
A learning summary, not legal advice.

      

    

    

      
Fairness check: does it treat groups alike?

      
Gender and age are deliberately left out of the model. That alone does not prove fairness, because other signals can stand in for them (receiving a pension is a stand-in for age). So we test the outcomes by group.

      

      

    

    

      
Monitoring: is today's data still like the training data?

      
Population stability index (PSI). Under 0.1 is stable, 0.1 to 0.25 needs a look, above 0.25 needs action.

      

      
Two of the alerts are expected and harmless: customers naturally get older with the bank, and a single month (September) has a seasonal pattern the multi-year training data averages out. Deciding which alerts matter is a human judgement, which is why monitoring needs an owner.

    

  


  
  

    

      

        
Stage 5 · Action

        
Today's call list for a relationship manager

        
The model is useless until it changes what someone does on Monday morning. This is what the front line sees: who to call, why, and what to say. Ranked by balance at risk, not just probability, so a likely drift on a large balance comes first.

      

      
Who does thisRelationship managers, branch and contact centre staff.Tools the CRM, where this list would live.Output calls made and outcomes logged, which feed back into stage 6.

Stage 6 · Proof

        
Did calling the list keep money in the bank?

        
We scored customers in September 1998 and then checked what really happened from October to December. That tells us how much drifting money the list would have reached. Whether a call actually saves it can only be proven with a live test.

      

      
Who does thisBusiness owners, strategy and finance.Tools controlled tests, dashboards, the budget.Output a decision: scale it, change it or stop it.

    

    

      

        

        

      

      

      

      
Drifting balance reached: the actual money that left (Oct to Dec 1998) from the accounts on the list. Random is the average of 200 random lists of the same size. The save rate is an assumption you control, not a measured result.

    

    

      

        
How a bank would prove it for real

        
    
          
  1. Take the top of the list and split it at random into two groups.
    2. 
          
  2. Call one group. Leave the other alone (the control group).
    3. 
          
  3. After three months, compare balances between the two groups.
    4. 
          
  4. The difference is the money the calls kept. Weigh it against staff time.
    5. 
        

        
Without the control group, you can't tell whether a customer stayed because of the call or would have stayed anyway.

      

      

        
Keep it working

        
    
          
  • Check stability every month (stage 4) and retrain when signals shift.
    • 
          
  • Log every call outcome so the next model learns which actions work.
    • 
          
  • Review fairness each quarter, not only at launch.
    • 
          
  • Retire the model if the controlled test shows no difference.
